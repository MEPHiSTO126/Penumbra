The questions of "what is, what should be and what should be done about what is". To begin, let's try to give some definition to the subject matters. First and foremost, "what is?" simply refers to what exists currently, or the current state of things. "What should be" refers to, supposedly, an expected state of things, or more accurately, a hypothetical most positive, current state of things. Before we dive into "what should be done about what is" let's take a deeper look at the statement "what should be". Perhaps, it's more of a question than a statement. Here, we can bring in the argument of "perspective". There are certain schools of thoughts, ideas or concepts that supposedly have a definitive "what should be". For example, should diseases exist at all? Arguably, the answer any human would give to this would be "No".

However, this answer is completely based on the overwhelming presence of the negative effects caused by the existence of diseases throughout the course of humanity. In this light, it would also be absurd to imagine that the absence of diseases could possibly produce any outcome more negative than that which already is. But again, if I dare say, there is absolutely no proof for such a claim and it is absolutely unknown what effect the supposed "absence of diseases" would cause. Of course, it is expected that there should be a more positive outcome. However, the point of emphasis is the uncertainty.

Imagine a hypothetical world entirely free of diseases. The only remaining causes of mortality would be conflict, starvation, accidents, and old age. Realistically, these factors alone are insufficient to naturally regulate the human population. The inevitable result would be an overwhelming demographic explosion. Consequently, the "ideal" disease-free world would likely devolve into a perpetual, desperate struggle among the populace for rapidly depleting fundamental resources. Ultimately, this would usher in an era of mass starvation and endless resource wars, which is a scenario that could reasonably be argued is far worse, and more systematically destructive, than the current reality of living alongside diseases.

Now then, regarding the question 'what should be done about what is?', let us further utilize the prior hypothetical. What should be done about the fact that diseases exist? This might possess some element of absurdism; however, given the earlier conclusion, it's a fair question. If there appears in the future a method that can erase all diseases from mankind, should humanity refuse it due to the possibility of a more adverse effect than what is current? That exactly is the question. However, the answer, unfortunately, in my opinion, does not exist. Taking a deeper look at the hypothetical and this question, we have derived the idea that the supposed ideal scenario of the absence of diseases is not guaranteed to be ideal. Yet, the current situation is also not a guaranteed ideal situation by the same metric, since we have no proof if another, or that same hypothetical scenario, would actually be more ideal. The question 'what should be done about what is?' is built upon the foundation of 'what should be?'. You only know the correct thing to do when you are aware of its supposedly positive consequences. The inherent problem, therefore, is that one cannot be certain of the supposedly positive result of an action; hence, one cannot be certain of the action to perform.
